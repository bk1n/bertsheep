bertsheep: ChemBERTa for EGFR binding affinity

Fine-tuning a pre-trained chemical language model on IC50 against wild-type human EGFR, compared with an XGBoost fingerprint baseline, in and out of distribution. 30 seeds per arm. Part of my work at Accenture Labs, refactored with Claude Code. Source: github.com/bk1n/bertsheep.

Summary

We trained ChemBERTa-10M-MTR to predict binding affinity (−ln IC50, nM) for 11,056 ligands of wild-type EGFR from BindingDB. It was trained either as a frozen encoder with a trained head or fully fine-tuned. We compared it with XGBoost on ECFP4 fingerprints and two reference predictors, across 30 Butina-cluster splits each in and out of distribution.

  • Every model learns chemistry beyond cluster identity. In distribution, all three beat predicting each molecule's cluster mean.
  • XGBoost on fingerprints beats ChemBERTa in distribution by 0.17–0.20 RMSE. Out of distribution its lead (0.12–0.13) is not significant.
  • Fine-tuning is no better than a frozen encoder. The difference is 0.03 RMSE in distribution and 0.01 out of distribution, and neither is significant.
  • Out of distribution, which clusters are held out matters more than which model is used. The spread across seeds is two to three times the gap between the best and worst model.
  • Fine-tuning reorganises the last encoder layer around affinity, but held-out clusters do not follow. Out of distribution, the test molecules stay near where they started.

ChemBERTa learns more than cluster identity. In distribution, every model beats the cluster-mean predictor by 0.29–0.49 RMSE. So the models are learning features of the compounds that predict binding to EGFR, not just recognising which chemical series a molecule belongs to.
XGBoost on fingerprints beats pre-trained and fine-tuned ChemBERTa. In distribution, XGBoost on ECFP4 is 0.17–0.20 RMSE better than either ChemBERTa arm. Out of distribution it is ahead on average by 0.12–0.13, but the gap is well within the seed-to-seed spread and not significant. Other authors have observed this. There are many possible reasons for the weaker performance, including negative transfer and limited dataset size.
Fine-tuning performs about the same as the pre-trained model. In distribution, fine-tuning is 0.03 RMSE better on average and better on 20 of 30 seeds, which is not significant (p = 0.22). Out of distribution the two are indistinguishable. One possible reason is the MTR pre-training objective: it regresses about 200 RDKit descriptors, so the frozen latent space may already encode affinity-relevant descriptors.
Out of distribution, which clusters are held out matters more than which model is used. Per-seed RMSEs are strongly correlated across the three models (r = 0.81–0.84).
  • Worst seed. Seed 24 is the worst for XGBoost, pre-trained and fine-tuned (RMSE 3.55, 3.25 and 3.62). On it, XGBoost and fine-tuning do worse than predicting the training mean (3.46).
  • Best seed. Seed 17 is the best for XGBoost and fine-tuned (1.96 and 2.09).
  • Spread versus gap. The spread across seeds (SD 0.31–0.38) is two to three times the gap between the best and worst model (0.13).
  • Test-set size. The test set ranges from 708 to 2,250 molecules, because clusters vary in size.
A single held-out split would give a misleading ranking of these models: some sets of clusters are simply easier to predict than others.

Question 2: how does fine-tuning change the latent space?

Aligned UMAP of the fine-tuned model's hidden states for seed 0, from the pre-trained weights through every epoch of fine-tuning. Columns are the embedding layer and encoder layers 2 and 3, and rows are in and out of distribution. The right-hand panel tracks the loss. Test molecules are drawn larger and opaque. Both videos are driven by the same controls.

0.0 s
  • The embedding layer barely moves. In both distributions its layout is nearly the same at the pre-trained weights and at the last epoch. Fine-tuning changes the encoder layers, not the token embeddings.
  • The last encoder layer reorganises around affinity. In distribution, by the selected epoch, weak binders (dark) collect on one side of encoder layer 3 and potent ones (yellow/orange) on the other. The same starts to happen out of distribution.
  • In distribution, test molecules move with their training neighbours. Test clusters share their series with training compounds, so they are carried along into the affinity-sorted layout.
  • Out of distribution, the training molecules rearrange while the held-out molecules stay close to where they started. The training molecules are still reorganised in the last layer into an affinity-sorted layout. The test clusters, in contrast, move little and do not follow them. The model is not learning generalisable features that transfer to these clusters, which matches its poor performance out of distribution.

Data

Labels come from the BindingDB dump BindingDB_All_202609_tsv. Four targets are registered for training. They were chosen on the number of Butina clusters of 10 or more, not on raw ligand count, because an out-of-distribution split needs enough whole clusters to hold out. All results here are for wild-type EGFR.

Methods

Models and baselines

ArmWhat it is
Training meanPredicts the training-set mean for every molecule: the floor every model has to clear.
Cluster meanPredicts the training mean of each molecule's Butina cluster: what knowing the series alone is worth. Out of distribution no test cluster is in training, so it falls back to the training mean and scores identically to it.
XGBoost + ECFP4XGBoost regressor on 1024-bit Morgan fingerprints (radius 2). Up to 5,000 trees, with early stopping after 50 rounds without improvement on validation.
Pre-trainedDeepChem/ChemBERTa-10M-MTR with its encoder frozen; only the regression head is trained.
Fine-tunedThe same model with the whole network trained (plain AdamW, no layerwise LR decay or layer reinitialisation).

Both ChemBERTa arms use a single-output regression head, with its bias initialised to the training mean. They are trained on MSE with batch size 128 and a linear warmup-then-decay schedule stepped per batch, for at most 80 epochs. Early stopping has a patience of 5 epochs on validation loss, and the epoch with the best validation loss is the one selected.

Splits

Compounds are clustered with Butina clustering on ECFP4 Tanimoto distance (cutoff 0.5), a relatively stringent method. Only clusters of 10 or more molecules enter the splits, which leaves 8,802 molecules. Each seed draws a new 70 / 15 / 15 train / validation / test split and a new model initialisation.

  1. In distribution: splitting is stratified by cluster (StratifiedKFold), so every cluster appears in train, validation and test.
  2. Out of distribution: whole clusters are held out (GroupShuffleSplit), so test molecules come from series never seen in training.

Tuning

Hyperparameters are optimised with Optuna's TPE sampler (10 random start-up trials) and a median pruner. The search uses 100 trials for XGBoost and 50 for each ChemBERTa arm. Because of the compute available, tuning runs on seed 0's split only, and every seed reuses the result.

Statistics

Arms are compared pairwise on per-seed test RMSE using Nadeau and Bengio's (2003) corrected resampled paired t-test. Every arm sees the same split for a given seed, so pairing on seed removes how hard each split is.

The correction is needed because the seeds are resplits of one dataset, not independent datasets. Their training sets overlap, so the per-seed differences are positively correlated. The plain paired t-test's variance, σ²/J, would therefore overstate certainty. Nadeau–Bengio replaces it with (1/J + ntest/ntrain) σ² and compares against t with J − 1 degrees of freedom.

p-values are adjusted for multiple comparisons with Holm's method, separately within each distribution. Out of distribution, the test-set size varies by seed and the ratio is averaged over seeds, so those p-values are approximate.

Compute

All runs were trained locally on a single NVIDIA RTX 4060. bf16 autocast (forward pass only, with weights and optimiser state in fp32) gave roughly a 2× speed-up. A median run takes about 40 s in distribution and 15–20 s out of distribution for the ChemBERTa arms, and under 10 s for XGBoost.

Reproduce

Setup, the BindingDB download and the training commands are in the README. uv run bertsheep EGFR --train --results reruns the tuning, the 30-seed grid and the figures in this report.
